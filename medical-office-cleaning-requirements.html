Medical Office Cleaning Requirements and Standards

Published by the ABR team · Updated October 2026

Key takeaways
  • Medical offices need stricter disinfection, training and documentation than ordinary offices.
  • OSHA's Bloodborne Pathogens and Hazard Communication standards apply, and regulated medical waste follows its own rules.
  • Clearly define who handles sharps and regulated waste. Janitorial crews generally do not.
Published by A Bright Rose Cleaning Services, a commercial-only cleaning company in West Palm Beach, FL serving Palm Beach County.

Medical and dental offices serve patients who may be vulnerable, and clinical areas must meet infection prevention expectations. A cleaning vendor supports that effort, but it does not replace your own infection control program. This is general information, not medical or legal advice.

Standards that commonly apply

  • OSHA Bloodborne Pathogens (29 CFR 1910.1030). Requires an exposure control plan, training, protective equipment and safe handling of contaminated items for employees with occupational exposure.
  • OSHA Hazard Communication (29 CFR 1910.1200). Labels, safety data sheets and training for chemicals.
  • CDC infection prevention guidance for healthcare settings. Practice-level guidance on environmental cleaning and disinfection.
  • State rules on biomedical waste. In Florida, biomedical waste is regulated by the Department of Health, and practices usually use a licensed hauler.
  • Your own infection control policies and any accreditation requirements.

What to require from a cleaning company

  • Documented training in bloodborne pathogens awareness and chemical safety
  • Use of EPA-registered disinfectants appropriate for the surface, used with the label's contact time
  • Color-coded tools to prevent cross-contamination between restrooms, exam rooms and common areas
  • A written scope that separates waiting room, restrooms, exam rooms and clinical areas
  • A clear boundary on regulated waste and sharps, so crews do not handle them unless trained and authorized
  • Confidentiality: crews must not read, move or photograph patient information
  • A verification method, such as checklists and inspections
  • Certificate of insurance and references from healthcare clients

Typical scope separation

  • Cleaning crew: waiting rooms, restrooms, floors, counters, exam room surfaces as agreed, trash (non-regulated), break rooms, high-touch points.
  • Clinical staff: instruments, sterilization, patient-care equipment and anything involving procedures.
  • Waste hauler: regulated medical waste and sharps containers.

Questions to ask

  1. What training do your crews have on bloodborne pathogens and chemical safety?
  2. Which disinfectants do you use and what are their contact times?
  3. What will your crew do if they find a needle or a blood spill?
  4. How do you protect patient privacy?
Practical advice: Provide your cleaning vendor with your exposure control plan information and site rules, and confirm what is out of bounds.
